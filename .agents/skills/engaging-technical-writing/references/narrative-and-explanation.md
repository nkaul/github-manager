# Narrative and explanation

Use for a new draft or structural revision.

## Establish a reader contract

Write a working brief: intended audience and prior knowledge; concrete task; supported friction; useful payoff; central claim and evidentiary limits. The opening should establish the task and outcome without specialist product terms. A small credible situation is enough. Label hypothetical scenes, and use first-person experience only when supplied or documented.

A useful sequence is situation → difficulty → visible solution → example → mechanism → evidence/tradeoffs → practical consequence. Adapt it to the genre. A research finding may need its result early; an architecture story may need a product walkthrough first. Do not manufacture a failed approach to complete a story arc.

## Build depth progressively

Outline the reader's information needs, not the project's file structure. For each section, identify what is now understood and what needs explaining next. Introduce technical vocabulary when it names an idea the reader can already picture.

Keep valuable depth. Add an orienting sentence, show a small example and explain the connection before increasing complexity. Do not fix a difficult explanation by removing the mechanism or moving all insight elsewhere. Companion artifacts hold complete data; the article retains the meaningful explanation and representative examples.

Repetition earns its place when it adds a connection or consolidates a difficult step. Paragraph length is a judgment about coherent ideas, not a sentence quota. Explain transitions through the reasoning itself instead of repeatedly announcing the article's structure.

## Make examples explain transformations

For a pipeline or algorithm, consider a stage table with input, operation, output and consequence. Show what is selected, removed, ranked or added. A named algorithm is not an explanation. Identify whether a number is a similarity, ranking score, probability, confidence or observed metric.

For AI workflows, separate retrieval, augmentation, generation, validation and application. Show policy/schema separately from source evidence. State whether a request is saved or reconstructed. Do not splice different experiments into a fictitious continuous trace. Reference answers never belong in model inputs.

For comparisons, preserve the task and input where possible. Explain what each approach solves, its costs and what evidence would change the choice. Mark untested alternatives as hypotheses.

## Use visuals at the point of need

Describe the point before the figure. Give each diagram one explanatory job. Small data examples and actual names help if legible. Captions explain the implication; alt text describes meaningful relationships. Match the user's established visual style, but check legacy diagram claims against current behavior.

## Preserve insight during revision

Maintain a coverage map from substantive source insights to the new draft. If the user asks to retain content, the article itself keeps its explanations; companion artifacts deepen them. A short social adaptation does not replace the complete version.
